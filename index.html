The Seaverman Files

Two Wrongs

A renowned criminal psychologist crosses over into vigilantism.

Dr. Marvin Seaverman, a criminal psychologist and police consultant, has always wanted to protect children, but when he cannot even protect his own daughter, Debbie, something breaks inside him. Feeling powerless and frustrated, Merv turns his fury against the very fiends his training has taught him to understand: abusers of children.

For over a decade, Merv has methodically catfished pedophiles, murdering the most egregious abusers, while successfully covering his tracks. All this time, the NYPD has remained oblivious to the existence of a vigilante serial killer in their city.

This week, Merv has slipped, and Detective Sharone Lazar of the 101st precinct—Debbie’s closest friend since childhood—has discerned the pattern. Sharone has not yet figured out the killer’s identity, but it is only a matter of time.

Doing What It Takes

A reclusive ex-mobster leads his protégé to the top of a local gang.

Jerry Kaufman was a Jewish gangster in 1950s Bronx. With a gift for strategy and a hard punch, Jerry was a rising star in the Italian mob, when he was betrayed and forced to abandon the life. Now a wheelchair-bound widower in his 90s, Jerry lives in near-isolation in a suburb just outside the Florida Everglades.

Looking back on his life, Jerry cannot shake the feeling that he missed his chance, so when he witnesses his caretaker’s son, Amede Delwa, beaten in a gang fight, Jerry sees an opportunity to try again, this time by proxy.

Amede is a Haitian man trying to make it in a Cuban gang, an outsider’s struggle Jerry viscerally understands. Sharp, strong, and charismatic, Amede has the skills to make it big. What he lacks in experience, Jerry can supply, along with money, connections, and strategy. But it will be up to Amede to do what it takes.

Walk With Me

A Poetry Collection

And though the way is mute,
Walk with me into the beautiful night
Where through the rough, quiet, and concealment
Each step melts away space made just for us.

Walk With Me is the debut poetry collection of Hila Bar, a South African-born writer, translator and editor who has lived in Israel since 1996. Moving between free verse, rhyme, haiku, and prose poetry, these more than eighty poems invite the reader along a quiet road at night, through landscape, memory, and inner musings.

The collection is arranged in seven sections. The Land travels through Israel’s desert, farmlands, coastal roads, and sea. Seasons and Time follows autumn rains, morning mist, bonfire nights, Remembrance Day and Independence Day, and the slow passing of years. Depression and Despair speaks honestly about loneliness and darkness, while Family and Fun turns to children, the first day of school, and the small comedies of home. Journey to Self explores silence, patience, faith, and the search for one’s own voice. Swords of Iron responds to the war that began in October 2023 and to its grief, from the unseasonal October rains to a mother’s loss. A final Miscellaneous section celebrates music, colour, cats, and the moment itself.

Written with a translator’s ear for language and a mother’s eye for the everyday, Bar’s poems hold beauty and heartache side by side. They find the sacred in a peeled carrot or a desert shadow, and they make room for sorrow without giving way to it.

Zev I. Farber

Non-fiction

New · 2026

Homosexual Relationships and Orthodox Judaism

Can Orthodox Jewish communities welcome gay couples and their families without setting aside halakha?

In 2012, Rabbi Zev Farber argued that asking gay Orthodox Jews to remain celibate for life was neither realistic nor compassionate. This book develops the halakhic argument behind that position. Farber examines the biblical and rabbinic sources on same-sex relations, surveys Orthodox responses to homosexuality, and revisits Rabbi Norman Lamm’s 1974 proposal to understand their situation through ʾônēs raḥmana paṭrei “God exempts those acting under duress.”

Building on Rabbi Meshulam Roth’s reading of a Talmudic case in which a woman is left with no permissible partner, Farber shows how a lack of a viable alternative can itself constitute ʾônēs. The question is not whether gay people can control their desires at any given moment, but whether halakha can demand that they forgo an intimate relationship for their entire lives.

Instead of pressuring homosexuals to be celibate, or demanding gay couples to negotiate a list of permitted acts and limit intimacy to moments of overwhelming need, Farber’s framework offers Orthodox communities a halakhic basis for welcoming gay couples and their children as families, without asking them to hide their relationships or live under communal judgment.
